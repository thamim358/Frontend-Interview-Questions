Section 1 — STAR Method Framework

Master the framework that every FAANG interviewer expects you to use.

S
Situation — Set the Scene
P1
What Interviewers Want

Describe a specific situation from your past experience. Be concrete: name the product, the team size, the timeline, and the stakes. Avoid hypotheticals — every story must be something you actually did.

Frontend Example

"In Q3 2023, our e-commerce checkout page had a 4.2-second Largest Contentful Paint on mobile. Our conversion rate was 11% below industry benchmark, and the VP of Product flagged this as the #1 priority for the quarter. I was the lead frontend engineer on a 6-person squad."

Pro Tip

Use specific numbers and real product names. Generic situations signal that you're making it up. Spend ~20% of your answer time on this section.

T
Task — Define Your Responsibility
P1
What Interviewers Want

Clearly state what you were personally responsible for. Use "I" not "we". If you were leading, say you were leading. If you were contributing, say what your specific piece was. Interviewers are evaluating your contribution, not your team's.

Frontend Example

"As the performance lead, my task was to get LCP under 2.5 seconds on mobile (down from 4.2s) within 6 weeks. I was also responsible for setting up performance monitoring so the team could catch regressions in CI before they reached production."

Mistake to Avoid

Using "we" throughout: "We identified the issue, we fixed it, we shipped." The interviewer cannot evaluate you. Replace every "we" with "I" and explain your actions.

A
Action — What You Did, Step by Step
P1
What Interviewers Want

This is the most important section. Walk through your specific actions in chronological order. Show your technical decision-making, collaboration, and ownership. This should be ~50% of your answer. Be granular — mention specific tools, techniques, and tradeoffs.

Frontend Example

"First, I ran Lighthouse audits and identified three bottlenecks: (1) a 2.1MB hero image loaded eagerly, (2) render-blocking CSS from Tailwind's full build, (3) a waterfall of 14 separate API calls for product data. I prioritized by impact: image optimization would save ~1.2s, CSS splitting ~0.7s, API batching ~0.5s. I created a technical proposal doc, got buy-in from the tech lead and PM, then executed: (1) switched to next/image with blurDataURL and WebP/AVIF formats, lazy-loaded below-fold images, (2) configured Tailwind purging with content paths and extracted critical CSS inline, (3) built a BFF (Backend For Frontend) endpoint that aggregated product data server-side. I added Lighthouse CI with budgets in our GitHub Actions pipeline to fail PRs that regressed LCP."

R
Result — Quantified Impact
P1
What Interviewers Want

Close with measurable results. Use numbers. If you can tie it to business impact (revenue, conversion, user engagement), even better. Also mention what you learned and how you shared that knowledge. Spend ~20% of answer time here.

Frontend Example

"Result: LCP dropped from 4.2s to 1.4s on mobile. Mobile conversion rate increased 8.3% within 2 weeks of the deploy. The Lighthouse CI setup caught 4 performance regressions before they merged in the following quarter. I wrote a performance playbook for the team and gave a lunch-and-learn to 40+ engineers. The VP of Product sent a company-wide shoutout. The BFF pattern got adopted by 3 other product teams."

Senior Insight

The "Result" section is where senior+ candidates differentiate themselves. Juniors say "it worked." Seniors say "it moved a business metric by X%" and show how they multiplied their impact by teaching others.

Section 2 — 8 Behavioral Questions with Full STAR Stories

Real frontend scenarios. Memorize the structure, not the words — make every story your own.

01
Tell me about a time you improved performance on a web application.
Advanced P1
Situation

At a SaaS analytics platform serving 50K daily users, our dashboard's initial load took 8+ seconds. Users were abandoning the page before the first chart rendered. Our NPS score dropped from 42 to 28, and the VP of Engineering declared this a "tier-1 incident" for the frontend team. I was a senior frontend engineer on a 5-person squad.

Task

I took ownership of the performance initiative. My target was to get Time to Interactive (TTI) under 3 seconds and reduce the JavaScript bundle by 60%. I also needed to establish ongoing performance monitoring so we'd catch regressions automatically.

Action

(1) I profiled the bundle with Webpack Bundle Analyzer and found three 800KB monsters: moment.js (full locale data), a charting library with all chart types imported, and a monolithic vendor chunk. (2) I replaced moment.js with date-fns (tree-shakable, saved 450KB). For the charting library, I implemented dynamic imports so each chart type loaded on demand. (3) I split the vendor chunk using SplitChunksPlugin with a custom cacheGroups strategy — separating React, the charting lib, and utilities into separate async chunks. (4) I implemented React.lazy + Suspense for 7 heavy dashboard widgets, reducing the initial JS payload by 62%. (5) To prevent regressions, I set up Web Vitals monitoring with web-vitals library sending data to Datadog, and added PerformanceObserver-based Long Task monitoring with threshold alerts. (6) I created a Webpack performance budget of 250KB initial JS — any PR exceeding it would fail CI.

Result

TTI dropped from 8.1s to 2.3s. Initial JS bundle went from 2.1MB to 795KB (62% reduction). User bounce rate on the dashboard page dropped 34%. NPS recovered to 45 within 6 weeks. The Webpack budget caught 7 potential regressions in the next quarter. I documented the entire approach in our internal wiki, and 2 other teams adopted the same lazy-loading + chunk-splitting strategy.

FAANG Angle

Amazon's "Dive Deep" principle demands performance stories like this. Meta's "Move Fast" values speed without breaking things — show how you balanced velocity with quality via automated budgets.

02
Describe a production bug you fixed. What was the root cause and how did you prevent it from happening again?
Expert P1
Situation

On a financial dashboard at a FinTech startup, we had a React application displaying real-time stock portfolio values. At 9:30 AM EST (market open), our on-call got paged — users were seeing stale portfolio values updated at random. Some users saw their own portfolio total, others saw completely different users' values. This was a P0 incident because it involved potential data leakage between user sessions.

Task

As the on-call engineer, I had to (1) identify the root cause within 30 minutes, (2) deploy a fix, (3) ensure no data was permanently leaked, and (4) implement safeguards to prevent this class of bug in the future.

Action

(1) I checked Datadog logs and saw the wrong user IDs in API responses — the server was returning correct data, so the bug was client-side. (2) I traced the issue to a stale closure in a setInterval callback. Our usePortfolio hook had a useEffect that set up a polling interval every 3 seconds. The interval callback captured the userId from its initial render via a closure. When users switched between portfolio tabs (different portfolioId), the old interval still ran with the initial userId and portfolioId. Multiple intervals accumulated — one per tab switch — all writing to the same global state. (3) I fixed it by using useRef for the portfolioId and reading ref.current inside the interval callback instead of relying on closure values. (4) I also added cleanup: the effect now clears the interval on portfolioId change and returns a cleanup function. (5) To prevent recurrence, I wrote an ESLint rule using eslint-plugin-react-hooks (already had it, but I wrote a custom rule that flagged setInterval inside useEffect as a warning) and added a section to our code review checklist: "Check for stale closures in async callbacks."

Result

Fix deployed within 45 minutes. Zero data leakage confirmed (the stale values were from the same session's previous portfolio, not cross-user). I gave a tech talk titled "Closures Are Not Your Friend in React Hooks" to our 25-person engineering team. The ESLint rule caught 4 similar patterns in other parts of the codebase during the following sprint. Incident post-mortem led to a new policy: any setInterval or setTimeout inside hooks requires explicit ref-based stale closure mitigation.

The Bug & Fix
// BUG: Stale closure captures initial portfolioId
function usePortfolio(portfolioId) {
  useEffect(() => {
    const interval = setInterval(() => {
      // portfolioId here is STALE — always the first value
      fetchPortfolio(portfolioId);
    }, 3000);
    return () => clearInterval(interval);
  }, []);  // Missing dependency!
}

// FIX: useRef to always read the latest value
function usePortfolio(portfolioId) {
  const idRef = useRef(portfolioId);
  idRef.current = portfolioId;

  useEffect(() => {
    const interval = setInterval(() => {
      fetchPortfolio(idRef.current);  // Always fresh
    }, 3000);
    return () => clearInterval(interval);
  }, []);  // Empty deps OK — ref handles staleness
}
03
Tell me about a conflict you had with a PM or Designer. How did you resolve it?
Intermediate P1
Situation

Our PM wanted to ship a new "AI-powered search" feature in 3 weeks for an upcoming investor demo. The design called for a real-time autocomplete with animated results, fuzzy matching, and voice input — all built from scratch. I estimated 8 weeks minimum for the frontend alone. The PM pushed back hard, saying the demo was non-negotiable. The designer had already created high-fidelity Figma mocks with complex micro-interactions.

Task

I needed to (1) deliver something impressive for the demo without compromising long-term code quality, (2) negotiate scope with the PM without burning the relationship, and (3) help the designer understand technical constraints without dismissing their vision.

Action

(1) Instead of saying "no," I proposed a phased approach: Phase 1 (3 weeks) — a polished MVP with typeahead using an existing library (Downshift), static mock data that looked real, and keyboard navigation. Phase 2 (post-demo) — real API integration, fuzzy search, voice input. (2) I created a comparison doc showing two columns: "Demo-Only (3 weeks)" vs "Production-Ready (8 weeks)" with a middle ground: "MVP + Incremental Polish." This made the tradeoffs visual and collaborative, not adversarial. (3) I sat with the designer for 30 minutes and walked through which micro-interactions would cost days vs hours. Together we identified 3 "high-impact, low-effort" animations that preserved 80% of the visual wow factor at 20% of the engineering cost. (4) I offered to personally polish the remaining interactions post-demo, which showed I valued their work.

Result

We shipped the MVP in 2.5 weeks — ahead of schedule. The investor demo went great; the search felt fast and polished. The PM publicly credited the engineering team for being "solution-oriented." The designer said it was the first time an engineer treated their work as a collaboration rather than a blocker. Post-demo, we shipped the full version in 5 additional weeks. The phased approach became our team's standard for all large features.

Key Lesson

Never say "no" to a PM. Say "yes, and here's how we can phase it." Frame constraints as tradeoffs, not roadblocks. When you bring solutions instead of problems, PMs become your biggest allies.

04
Describe a time you mentored a junior developer.
Intermediate P1
Situation

A new grad joined our team — bright, eager, but overwhelmed by React hooks, TypeScript generics, and our complex monorepo. She was assigned a task to build a form wizard with 5 steps, conditional validation, and file upload. After 2 weeks, her PR had 87 comments and she looked defeated. The sprint was at risk and she was talking about whether frontend was "right for her."

Task

As the senior engineer, I needed to get the feature shipped while building her confidence and skills — not just fix the code for her. I also wanted to improve our onboarding process so this pattern wouldn't repeat.

Action

(1) I set up structured pair programming: 1 hour daily, driver/navigator rotation. First session, I drove while explaining my thought process (why I reach for useReducer over useState for multi-step forms, why zod over Yup for schema validation, why React Hook Form over manual state management). By session 3, she was driving and I was asking questions to guide her reasoning. (2) I broke the task into 5 micro-PRs — each one < 200 lines. First PR: just the step navigation with a type-safe enum. Second: one form field with validation. By PR 4, she was submitting independently. (3) I gave feedback in sandwich format: praise, specific improvement, encouragement. Never "this is wrong" — always "what if we considered this edge case?" (4) I created a "First Month Roadmap" doc for future hires: 10 small wins (fix a typo, add a test, ship a small component) designed to build momentum.

Result

The form wizard shipped on time with 0 bugs. Within 3 months, she was independently shipping features and reviewing other juniors' code. She became the team's "forms expert" and wrote our internal form patterns guide. The onboarding roadmap I created reduced time-to-first-PR for the next 3 hires from 3 weeks to 4 days. She later told me that the structured pairing sessions were the moment she "fell in love with frontend."

Senior Insight

Mentoring isn't charity — it's a leadership signal. Amazon's "Hire and Develop the Best" principle explicitly evaluates this. The best mentors don't give answers; they ask questions that lead juniors to discover the answers themselves.

05
Tell me about a time you made a wrong technical decision. What did you learn?
Advanced P1
Situation

At a Series B startup, I was the first frontend hire building our B2B admin dashboard. I chose Redux for state management because "every serious app uses Redux" — this was 2021, pre-React Query mainstream adoption. We had pages that did nothing but fetch data, show a table, and let admins filter. Every feature required: action types, action creators, reducers, selectors, thunks — sometimes 5 files for a single API call.

Task

6 months in, we had 40+ Redux files, a 200KB Redux store, and every new feature took 3x longer than it should. The team had grown to 4 engineers, and all of them struggled with the Redux boilerplate. I needed to (1) acknowledge my mistake, (2) propose a migration path, and (3) ensure we didn't lose velocity during the transition.

Action

(1) I wrote an honest post-mortem doc: "We chose Redux for all state when 80% of our state is server cache." I admitted my mistake openly in a team meeting — no defensiveness, just data. This built trust. (2) I proposed React Query for server state and Zustand for the remaining client state (theme, filters, UI toggles). (3) I did not propose a big-bang migration. Instead: all new features use the new stack. Existing Redux code stays until it needs changes, then we migrate that slice opportunistically. (4) I paired with each team member on their first React Query feature so nobody felt left behind.

Result

New feature velocity increased ~2.5x. The codebase shrank by 12K lines (deleted Redux boilerplate) over 4 months. Team morale improved — engineers stopped dreading state management tasks. I learned that choosing a tool because it's popular is a trap; choose the tool that solves your specific problem. React Query for server cache, Zustand for client state, nothing for local component state — that's the heuristic I now teach every new team member.

The Real Mistake

Not choosing Redux — choosing it without questioning whether it fit the problem. The lesson: always ask "what kind of state is this?" before choosing a state management tool. Server cache ≠ client state ≠ UI state.

06
Describe a project you architected from scratch. Walk me through your decisions.
Expert P1
Situation

Our company decided to rebuild the customer-facing dashboard as a greenfield project — migrating from a legacy AngularJS monolith to a modern React + Next.js stack. I was the tech lead tasked with the architecture. The requirements: serve 200K daily active users, support i18n for 12 languages, real-time data updates via WebSocket, role-based access control (RBAC) across 5 user roles, and a component library shared with 3 other products.

Task

I needed to design the project structure, choose the tech stack, establish conventions, and ensure the architecture could scale to 10+ developers contributing simultaneously without merge conflicts.

Action

Tech Stack Decision: Next.js 14 (App Router) for SSR/SSG hybrid rendering, TypeScript (strict mode), Tailwind CSS + Radix UI for the component library (accessibility-first), React Query for server state, Zustand for client state, and a shared Turborepo monorepo for the component library across products.

Folder Structure:

Project Architecture
src/
├── app/                    // Next.js App Router (routing only)
│   ├── (auth)/            // Route group: authenticated routes
│   │   ├── dashboard/
│   │   └── settings/
│   ├── (public)/           // Route group: public routes
│   └── api/               // Route handlers
├── features/              // Feature-sliced: each feature is self-contained
│   ├── auth/
│   │   ├── components/
│   │   ├── hooks/
│   │   ├── api/           // React Query hooks for this feature
│   │   └── types/
│   ├── billing/
│   └── analytics/
├── shared/                // Shared across features
│   ├── components/        // Button, Modal, Table etc.
│   ├── hooks/             // useDebounce, useMediaQuery etc.
│   ├── utils/
│   └── lib/              // Third-party wrappers
└── config/                // App-wide constants, env schema
Key Architectural Decisions

Feature-Sliced Design: Each feature folder is self-contained — components, hooks, types, and API calls live together. This eliminated the classic "components/components/" deep nesting problem and meant 10 devs could work on 10 features with zero merge conflicts. Barrel exports: Every feature exports via index.ts — no deep imports across feature boundaries. API layer: All API calls go through React Query hooks with a typed apiClient wrapper. No raw fetch calls in components. i18n: Used next-intl with namespaced JSON files per feature — each feature owns its own translations. RBAC: A PermissionGuard component wrapping a usePermissions hook — declarative, composable, no imperative role checks scattered across the codebase.

Result

Shipped MVP in 10 weeks with 4 engineers. The feature-sliced design scaled to 14 engineers without architectural churn. The shared component library saved an estimated 300+ developer-hours across 3 products in the first year. Lighthouse score: 98 performance, 100 accessibility. The architecture got referenced in the company's internal engineering blog as the "blueprint for future greenfield projects."

07
How do you handle tight deadlines?
Intermediate P2
Situation

Our CEO committed to a major client that we'd deliver a white-labeled version of our SaaS product in 4 weeks — for a contract worth $2M ARR. The problem: white-labeling required a dynamic theming system, custom domain support, per-client feature flags, and isolated data — none of which existed. The engineering team estimated 12 weeks.

Task

I volunteered to lead the frontend white-labeling work. I had to (1) negotiate what "white-label" meant for this specific client vs our long-term vision, (2) prioritize ruthlessly, (3) keep the team motivated through a high-pressure sprint, and (4) communicate risks transparently.

Action

(1) I met with the client success manager and clarified: the client only needed CSS token overrides (colors, fonts, logos) and a custom domain for the launch — not full white-labeling. (2) I created a MoSCoW prioritization matrix: Must-have (CSS variables theming + custom domain), Should-have (feature flags), Could-have (custom email templates), Won't-have (multi-tenant database isolation — phase 2). (3) I built the MVP approach: CSS custom properties injected via a JSON config fetched at app initialization. Theme tokens: --color-primary, --font-family, --brand-logo — everything in a CSS-in-TS token provider. (4) I ran daily 15-minute standups (not the full scrum ceremony — just blockers and progress), and updated the CEO and client success team every Monday with a "confidence score": Green/Yellow/Red. When we hit a DNS propagation delay in week 3, I flagged it as Red immediately — no sugar-coating.

Result

Delivered the white-labeled MVP in 3.5 weeks — 3 days early. The client signed the $2M contract. The CSS token system became our permanent theming infrastructure, later used by 15+ white-label clients. My transparent communication approach earned trust with the CEO, who started looping me into pre-sale engineering estimates afterward.

Takeaway

Deadlines feel tight when scope isn't negotiated. The skill isn't "working faster" — it's clarifying what "done" actually means. MVP scope + transparent communication = trust even when dates slip.

08
Tell me about a time you influenced without authority.
Advanced P2
Situation

Our product had 3 separate frontend teams (Dashboard, Mobile Web, Admin) each building their own button components, modal implementations, and form validation patterns. Accessibility was inconsistent — the Dashboard team had proper ARIA labels, Mobile Web had none, Admin had some but broken on screen readers. There was no mandate from leadership to unify; the CTO considered it "nice to have."

Task

I wanted to create a shared component library that all 3 teams would actually use. But I had no authority over the other teams — their tech leads reported to different EMs. I needed to influence without mandate.

Action

(1) I didn't start by building. I started by collecting data: I ran an accessibility audit (axe-core + Lighthouse) across all 3 products and quantified the gaps: 47 accessibility violations in Mobile Web, 23 in Admin. I turned this into a 1-page doc titled "What our disabled users experience today." (2) I didn't propose a mandatory migration. I built a single component (Button) with full accessibility, theming, loading states, and TypeScript autocomplete — then showed it to the Mobile Web tech lead with a demo: "I built this. If your team uses it, you get accessibility for free. Here's a 5-minute migration guide. If not, no pressure." (3) The Mobile Web lead tried it, loved the TypeScript DX, and migrated their buttons in a week. (4) That success created a pull dynamic — the Admin team saw Mobile Web ship faster with fewer bugs and asked to use the library. (5) I pitched the CTO with data: "Teams using the shared library ship components 40% faster with 0 accessibility regressions." Got approval for a dedicated "Design Systems" working group with me as the lead.

Result

Within 6 months, all 3 teams adopted the component library. Accessibility violations dropped 92% across products. I was promoted to Staff Engineer with "Design Systems" as my charter. The approach became our org's playbook for cross-team initiatives: prove value with a small win, let success create demand, then ask for resources — never the reverse.

FAANG Angle

This directly maps to Amazon's "Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit" and "Deliver Results" principles. Google values "influence without authority" as a core Staff+ competency.

Section 3 — Company-Specific Preparation

Every FAANG company has a distinct interview culture. Tailor your stories accordingly.

AMZN
Amazon — Leadership Principles Mapped to Frontend
Advanced P1
How Amazon Interviews Work

Every Amazon interviewer is assigned 1-2 Leadership Principles (LPs) to evaluate. Your behavioral answers MUST demonstrate LPs. Technical skills are table stakes; LP alignment determines hire/no-hire. You need 2 stories per LP ready. The "Bar Raiser" interviewer has veto power over the hiring decision.

Customer Obsession — Frontend Examples

Story angle: A feature you built specifically because of user feedback or data showing user pain. Frontend example: "Our analytics showed 23% of mobile users dropped off at the checkout because the form required 14 fields. I A/B tested a progressive disclosure approach — only 4 fields shown initially, rest revealed contextually. Conversion increased 17%." Red flag to avoid: "I built what the PM told me to build" — Amazon wants you to advocate for the customer, not just execute.

Dive Deep — Frontend Examples

Story angle: A bug or performance issue where you went beyond surface symptoms to root cause. Frontend example: "Memory leak causing OOM crashes after 30 minutes of usage — I used Chrome DevTools heap snapshots, identified a detached DOM tree from a charting library that wasn't cleaning up ResizeObserver on unmount. Fixed the cleanup in the library's wrapper, contributed the fix upstream."

Deliver Results — Frontend Examples

Story angle: A project where you shipped despite obstacles. Frontend example: "Black Friday deadline — our payment provider's SDK was incompatible with our SSR setup. I reverse-engineered the SDK's API calls, built a thin client wrapper that worked with both server-side render and client-side hydration. Shipped 2 days before Black Friday. Zero payment failures during peak traffic."

Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit — Frontend Examples

Story angle: A time you disagreed with a technical decision, respectfully pushed back, then committed fully once the decision was made. Frontend example: "Our architect wanted to use GraphQL for a dashboard that had 3 REST endpoints. I argued the added complexity (Apollo Client cache, fragment matching, query batching) wasn't justified for 3 endpoints. Presented data on bundle size impact (+45KB) and developer onboarding time (+2 weeks). We disagreed, architect made the final call to use REST. I committed fully — built the REST client with React Query, shipped on time, the dashboard performs great."

Memory Trick: Prepare 16 stories total — 2 per LP. Amazon typically tests 6-8 LPs per loop. The most frequently tested LPs for frontend roles: Customer Obsession, Dive Deep, Deliver Results, Have Backbone, Learn and Be Curious, Bias for Action, Insist on the Highest Standards.
GOOG
Google — Googleyness, Product Sense & Ambiguity
Expert P1
How Google Interviews Work

Google evaluates 4 attributes: (1) General Cognitive Ability (problem-solving, not just memorization), (2) Role-Related Knowledge (frontend specifics), (3) Leadership (influence, mentorship, cross-team impact), (4) Googleyness (comfort with ambiguity, collaboration, user-first thinking). The "Googleyness" round is behavioral-first but expects product thinking.

Googleyness — Frontend Angle

Google wants to see: (a) You thrive in ambiguity — given a vague product spec, you can define the problem, propose solutions, and iterate. (b) You think user-first — not "what's the coolest tech" but "what solves the user's actual problem." (c) You collaborate across functions — UX researchers, PMs, backend engineers, SREs. Story angle: "The PM said 'build a notification center' with zero details. I interviewed 5 power users, identified that the real pain point was notification overload (40+ per day), and built a smart grouping system that collapsed similar notifications. User satisfaction score went from 3.2 to 4.6."

Product Sense Questions

Google often asks frontend candidates product design questions: "How would you design a new Gmail compose experience? Walk me through your thought process." Framework: (1) Clarify: Who's the user? What's the problem? What constraints exist? (2) User journey: What does the user need to accomplish? Map the flow. (3) Prioritize: What's the MVP? What can wait? (4) Technical feasibility: Frontend constraints — latency, offline support, accessibility. (5) Metrics: How would you measure success? (6) Edge cases: Error states, empty states, loading states, accessibility.

Scalable Systems Thinking

Google expects frontend engineers to think about scale: "How would you build a chat feature that works for 100M users across 150 countries?" Cover: (a) Performance — code splitting, lazy loading, CDN edge caching, (b) i18n/l10n — RTL support, locale-aware formatting, translation pipeline, (c) Accessibility — WCAG 2.1 AA minimum, screen reader testing, keyboard navigation, (d) Offline — Service Worker caching, optimistic UI, conflict resolution, (e) Observability — error boundaries, performance monitoring, user analytics.

Senior Insight

Google's L5+ (Senior) bar for frontend requires demonstrating technical leadership beyond your team. Prepare stories about: (a) cross-team architecture decisions you drove, (b) how you improved engineering quality org-wide, (c) mentoring that scaled beyond 1:1 (internal talks, documentation, tools).

META
Meta — Move Fast, Impact at Scale, Product Design
Expert P1
How Meta Interviews Work

Meta has a structured behavioral round (often with a hiring manager) focused on: (1) Impact at scale — your work affected millions of users or materially moved metrics, (2) Move Fast — you ship fast, iterate, learn from data, (3) Product design — you can design features for Facebook/Instagram/WhatsApp scale, (4) Conflict resolution — you handle disagreements constructively. Meta interviewers use a rubric — every answer is scored against predefined criteria.

"Design a Facebook Feature" Questions

Classic Meta questions: "Design Facebook Stories" / "Design Instagram Reels" / "Design WhatsApp Voice Messages." Your answer must show product thinking not just frontend implementation:

  • User problem: What job does this feature do for the user?
  • UX flow: Tap by tap — what does the user see and do?
  • Technical architecture: Component tree, data flow, API layer
  • Performance at scale: Optimistic UI, lazy loading, CDN strategy
  • Offline support: What happens with spotty connection?
  • A11y: Screen reader, keyboard nav, reduced motion
  • Metrics: What defines success? Engagement? Creation rate? Time spent?
  • Edge cases: Very long content, very short content, zero content, RTL languages
Impact at Scale Stories

Meta cares about magnitude of impact — millions of users, measurable metric improvements. Story angle: "I rebuilt the search autocomplete from a debounced input to a client-side trie-based index with Web Worker processing. This reduced search latency from 400ms to 8ms for 90th percentile queries. A/B test showed 12% more searches completed, driving 3M additional monthly queries across 50M MAU."

Move Fast Stories

Meta values speed of execution. Story angle: "We needed an MVP of the onboarding flow for a new market launch in 1 week. I used an existing design system, hard-coded 3 screens instead of building a CMS (fast follow later), and shipped a working flow in 4 days. We learned from real user data that 2 of the 3 screens were unnecessary — saving us from building a complex feature nobody needed."

Meta-Specific Mistake

Don't say "we built it perfectly the first time." Meta values iteration and learning from data. A story where you shipped fast, learned, and iterated is 10x better than one where you took 6 months to build the "right" thing.

Accessibility at Scale

Cover: (a) Feature flags — LaunchDarkly/in-house, (b) Metrics — engagement (% who start watching), discovery (browse depth), satisfaction (thumbs up rate), (c) Statistical significance — sample size, duration, confidence level, (d) No flicker — assign variant server-side, hydrate without layout shift, (e) Ramp-up strategy — 1% → 10% → 50% → 100%, monitor metrics at each stage.

Section 4 — Senior/Staff Level Questions

Questions that separate Senior from Staff engineers — leadership, quality, and strategic thinking.

S1
How do you drive technical decisions across teams?
Expert P1
The Interviewer Is Evaluating

Your ability to (a) identify when a decision needs cross-team alignment vs when a team can decide autonomously, (b) facilitate decision-making without becoming a bottleneck, (c) build consensus without authority, (d) document and communicate decisions so they stick.

Model Answer

When we needed to standardize our API client across 4 frontend teams: (1) I wrote an RFC (Request for Comments) document outlining the problem (4 different HTTP clients — axios, fetch, ky, got), the proposed solution (a shared @acme/api-client package), alternatives considered, and migration path. (2) I shared it with all tech leads, gave them 1 week to comment, and incorporated feedback (some teams needed React Native support — I added it). (3) Instead of mandating adoption, I built the client with a migration guide showing "before/after" for 3 common patterns (GET, POST, error handling). (4) I personally helped each team migrate their first endpoint — removing friction. (5) I set up a bi-weekly "Frontend Guild" meeting where tech leads discuss cross-cutting decisions — the API client was just the first topic.

Key principle: Write it down (RFC), ask for feedback, make it easy to adopt, don't mandate — demonstrate value.

Staff+ Differentiator

Juniors make decisions alone. Seniors make decisions with their team. Staff engineers create decision-making frameworks that scale beyond themselves. The RFC process IS the framework.

S2
How do you build a quality-first culture?
Advanced P2
Model Answer

Culture is what the team does when nobody is watching. I build quality culture through:

(1) Automation over discipline: People forget things. Linters, type-checkers, and CI checks never forget. I set up ESLint (strict config), TypeScript strict mode, and pre-commit hooks that run lint + typecheck. The CI fails if coverage drops below 80%. This removes the "should I write tests?" decision — the pipeline decides.

(2) Developer Experience as quality: Slow CI or flaky tests erode quality culture. I optimized our CI from 18 min to 4 min (parallel jobs, caching). When tests caught a real bug, I celebrated it publicly in Slack — reinforcing that quality is valued.

(3) Code review as learning, not policing: I wrote a code review guide with specific patterns to check (error states, loading states, edge cases) instead of style nitpicks. Style is automated by Prettier. Reviews focus on logic, accessibility, and performance.

(4) Blameless post-mortems: When bugs reach production, the response is "how did our process allow this?" not "who wrote this?" I led 3 post-mortems that turned into process improvements (new ESLint rules, better error boundary coverage, canary deploys).

S3
How do you balance innovation vs stability?
Expert P2
Model Answer

This is a false dichotomy if you architect for both. My approach:

(1) Separate the stable from the experimental: Core infrastructure (auth, routing, API client) follows strict change management — RFCs, gradual rollout, feature flags with kill switches. Experimental features (new UI patterns, new libraries) are evaluated in isolation with feature flags and A/B testing. Facebook's "Gatekeeper" system is the model: ship code dark, enable for 1% of users, monitor, ramp up.

(2) Strangler Fig pattern for migrations: Don't rewrite, replace incrementally. When migrating AngularJS to React, we ran both frameworks side by side via a bridge component. New features in React, old code untouched until it needed changes. Zero downtime, zero lost velocity.

(3) Innovation time: I advocated for "Hack Days" every 6 weeks — 1 day where the team explores new tech, prototypes ideas, or fixes tech debt. Multiple production features (our dark mode, our virtualized table component) started as Hack Day projects. This channels innovation into a safe, time-boxed container.

(4) Measure both: Track innovation metrics (new features shipped, experiments run, time-to-prototype) AND stability metrics (error rate, P99 latency, uptime). Present both in sprint reviews. Teams optimize what gets measured.

Key Principle

Innovation without stability is chaos. Stability without innovation is stagnation. The answer is architectural isolation: make it safe to experiment by ensuring experiments can't break the stable core.
